\section{Introduction}
Association Rule Mining (ARM) is a fundamental technique in Knowledge Discovery in Databases (KDD) for identifying relationships and co-occurrence patterns among variables in large datasets. Originally developed for transactional analysis, ARM has become an important pattern-discovery approach because it can identify combinations of features that occur together without requiring a predefined parametric model. Algorithms such as Apriori and FP-Growth made it possible to systematically search large combinatorial spaces for frequent itemsets and derive association rules from them. Although traditionally associated with applications such as market-basket analysis, the ability of ARM to discover multi-feature relationships has motivated its application to more complex domains, including biomedical research and genomics. In these domains, associations among multiple genes, molecular markers, or phenotypic characteristics may provide useful insights that are difficult to capture using analyses focused on individual variables.

Genomic and transcriptomic datasets, however, differ substantially from the transactional datasets for which classical ARM was originally developed. High-throughput technologies such as microarrays can measure the expression levels of thousands of genes across a relatively small number of biological samples, resulting in a high-dimensional setting in which the number of features (p) can greatly exceed the number of observations (n) $((p \gg n))$. Microarray measurements are also continuous and therefore generally require preprocessing or discretization before conventional association-rule algorithms can be applied. Despite these challenges, the structure of such data makes association mining potentially attractive. Biological phenomena may depend on combinations of genes rather than isolated features, and interactions among multiple genes may contain information that is not apparent from univariate analysis. Consequently, ARM provides a natural framework for investigating recurring combinations of gene-expression states and their associations with biological or clinical characteristics.

The same characteristics that make high-dimensional genomic data attractive for pattern mining also create substantial computational and analytical challenges. Classical ARM algorithms operate over a combinatorial search space whose size can increase rapidly with the number of available items. In a microarray dataset containing thousands of genes, even a relatively small fraction of genes can produce an enormous number of possible itemsets. The resulting search can lead to excessive execution time and memory consumption, while stringent minimum-support thresholds may eliminate potentially interesting patterns. Conversely, relaxing the support threshold can cause a rapid increase in the number of frequent itemsets and association rules, producing a large volume of redundant, weak, or difficult-to-interpret patterns. Thus, the central challenge is not simply whether an ARM algorithm can generate rules from genomic data, but whether it can do so within practical computational limits while producing patterns that remain analytically useful.

\section{Dataset and Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset}

The dataset used in this study is a publicly available Affymetrix HG-U133A microarray
gene expression dataset for breast cancer, comprising raw probe-level intensity values
alongside clinical annotations for each patient sample.

\subsubsection{Biological context}
Each sample corresponds to a primary breast tumor profiled at diagnosis, with the
clinical outcome of interest being disease relapse. Gene expression from microarray
probes serves as a proxy for the transcriptional state of the tumor, under the
hypothesis that differential expression of specific genes is associated with relapse
risk. This is a well-studied outcome-prediction problem in the breast cancer genomics
literature, motivating the use of expression-derived rules as an interpretable
complement to black-box classifiers.

\paragraph{AFFX control probes.} The HG-U133A array includes a set of AFFX-prefixed
probes used for platform-level quality control (e.g., spike-in and housekeeping
controls). These do not represent biologically variable gene expression relevant to
relapse and were removed prior to any downstream analysis.

\paragraph{Gene expression (genetic) features.} The bulk of the feature space —
22{,}215 probes after control-probe removal — consists of continuous log-intensity
expression values, each corresponding to a distinct gene or transcript. These are the
primary features of interest and the sole target of the mutual-information ranking
and gene-subset selection described in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\paragraph{Clinical covariates.} Two clinically annotated covariates,
\texttt{lymph\_node\_status} and \texttt{ER\_Status} (estrogen receptor status), were
retained alongside the expression features. Both are established prognostic factors
in breast cancer and were kept to allow the ARM phase to surface rules combining
molecular and clinical evidence, though the current study's ARM results
(Section~\ref{sec:arm-results}) are reported over gene-expression-only itemsets, with
clinical covariate integration noted as future work.

\paragraph{Leakage-risk columns.} Three columns — \texttt{GEO\_accession\_number},
\texttt{time\_to\_relapse}, and \texttt{Brain\_relapses} — were excluded because they
either directly encode outcome-adjacent information (leaking the relapse label) or
carry no generalizable biological signal (a sample identifier). Their removal is
detailed in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\paragraph{Target label.} The \texttt{relapse} column is the binary outcome of
interest. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}, this was one-hot encoded
into \texttt{relapse\_yes} / \texttt{relapse\_no} items rather than left as a bare
binary column, a correction that was necessary for the minority class to be mineable
under standard ARM itemization.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}
Raw microarray intensity data is continuous, high-dimensional, and heavily
platform-annotated — none of which is directly compatible with classical
transaction-based Association Rule Mining. Converting this data into a valid ARM
input required several deliberate preprocessing decisions, described below.

\subsubsection{Filtering and leakage removal}
AFFX control probes (spike-in controls used for platform quality assessment rather
than biological signal) were removed, as were columns that would leak the outcome
directly: \texttt{GEO\_accession\_number}, \texttt{time\_to\_relapse}, and
\texttt{Brain\_relapses}. Clinical covariates with plausible prognostic value
(\texttt{lymph\_node\_status}, \texttt{ER\_Status}) were retained alongside the
\texttt{relapse} target. This yielded a cleaned matrix of $286 \times 22{,}215$
expression features plus covariates and target.

\subsubsection{Normalization}
No additional normalization was applied beyond the platform-provided
log-intensity values in the source series-matrix file; expression values
were used as-is prior to discretization.

\subsubsection{Missing values}
A completeness check on the merged expression matrix confirmed no missing
measurements across all 286 samples; no imputation was required.

\subsubsection{Gene selection and dimensionality variants}
Given $p = 22{,}215 \gg n = 286$ (a feature-to-sample ratio of roughly
$78{:}1$), exhaustive ARM over the full expression space is computationally
intractable and statistically unreliable. Gene selection was therefore
performed as a supervised filter step prior to mining, using three
class-aware ranking methods computed against the binary \texttt{relapse}
label: mutual information (MI, primary method; scores range $0$--$0.119$,
median $\approx 0.019$), chi-square ($\chi^2$ up to $12.55$, computed on the
same discretized features as MI), and a two-sample $t$-test on the
\emph{continuous} intensities ($|t|$ up to $4.89$), which avoids the
information loss of discretization at the cost of comparing means only.

\subsubsection{Discretization of continuous expression values}
Each of the 22{,}215 expression probes was converted independently into a
binary item via a per-probe median split, using strict inequality:
\begin{equation}
x_{ij}' = \begin{cases} 1 & x_{ij} > \mathrm{median}(x_{\cdot j}) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
so that values equal to the probe's median fall into the low bin. This
produces an approximately, but not exactly, balanced split per gene (observed
high-bin support in $[0.489, 0.500]$ across selected genes). The split is
unsupervised with respect to \texttt{relapse} and, as noted in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, discards magnitude information and can
inflate spurious co-occurrences for genes whose values cluster near the
median — a deliberate first-pass baseline rather than a final discretization
method.

\subsubsection{Conversion into transactions and items}
Following discretization, each sample was represented as a transaction —
a set of categorical items — following the template:
\[
\text{Sample}_i = \{\text{Gene}_1\_\text{HIGH},\ \text{Gene}_7\_\text{LOW},\
\text{Gene}_{42}\_\text{HIGH},\ \ldots\}
\]
applied independently to each selected gene subset, producing transaction
databases of corresponding width for the ARM experiments in
Section~\ref{sec:arm-results}.

\subsubsection{Label handling}
The target label required particular care during itemization. An initial
implementation encoded \texttt{relapse} as a single bare binary column,
which under standard itemization structurally suppressed rule discovery on
the minority (relapse) side. This was corrected by one-hot encoding the
target into two explicit items, \texttt{relapse\_yes} and
\texttt{relapse\_no}, each usable as a candidate consequent under the
class-relative support thresholds described in Section~\ref{sec:arm-results}.

\section{Methodology and Pipeline}

The pipeline was implemented as two clean, non-overlapping stages, following a design principle that downstream scripts should not duplicate logic already handled upstream.

\subsection{Stage 1: Data Cleaning (\texttt{clean\_features.py})}

This stage reads the raw microarray data and produces \texttt{cleaned\_data.csv}, containing 286 samples and 22{,}215 expression probes after removing AFFX control probes and the identified leakage columns. This script also defines shared constants (\texttt{CLINICAL\_COLS}, \texttt{TARGET\_COL}) that are imported, rather than redefined, by later stages.

\subsection{Stage 2: Feature Selection (\texttt{feature\_selection.py})}

This stage reads the already-cleaned data (it does not re-run any cleaning logic) and performs the following:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Discretization}: Each expression probe is discretized via a per-feature median split, producing a binary high/low indicator for each gene.
    \item \textbf{Mutual information scoring}: Mutual information (MI) between each discretized feature and the binary \texttt{relapse} label is computed using \texttt{sklearn.feature\_selection.mutual\_info\_classif} \citep{pedregosa2011scikit}.
    \item \textbf{Ranking and subset export}: Features are ranked by MI score, written to \texttt{gene\_mi\_rankings.csv}, and the top-100, top-500, and top-1000 gene subsets are exported to \texttt{data/final/} for downstream ARM experiments.
\end{enumerate}

Median-split discretization was adopted as a simple, interpretable first approach. However, it is a known and documented limitation: by construction it forces exactly 50\% support for every discretized feature, which destroys differential expression signal and can inflate spurious associations. This is treated as a deliberate, acknowledged constraint of the current phase rather than a defect to be corrected mid-project.

\subsection{Class Association Rule Mining}

Class Association Rule Mining (CARM) was performed on the top-100 gene subset using the FP-Growth algorithm as implemented in \texttt{mlxtend} \citep{raschka2018mlxtend}, which is a more efficient alternative to the original Apriori algorithm \citep{agrawal1994fast} for frequent itemset mining.

\section{Results}
\label{sec:arm-results}

\subsection{Mutual Information Scores}

MI scores for the ranked genes fell in the range of approximately 0.0 to 0.119. These values are low in absolute terms but are consistent with expectations given the coarseness of median-split discretization combined with a relatively small sample size ($n = 286$) for a high-dimensional feature space.

\subsection{CARM on the Top-100 Gene Subset}

Applying FP-Growth-based CARM to the top-100 MI-ranked genes, using a global minimum support threshold of 0.25, yielded the following results:

\begin{itemize}
    \item Only 8 rules were found with lift greater than 1.2.
    \item All 8 rules predicted the \texttt{relapse\_no} class.
    \item Average bootstrap stability across these rules was approximately 46\%.
    \item Zero rules predicting \texttt{relapse\_yes} (the minority class) were discovered.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Diagnosis: The Zero-Minority-Rule Problem}

The complete absence of \texttt{relapse\_yes} rules was investigated and attributed to a threshold artifact rather than a genuine absence of signal. A single global minimum support threshold of 0.25, applied uniformly across the dataset, structurally disadvantages the minority class: if the minority class constitutes a smaller fraction of the 286 samples, itemsets confined to that class are much less likely to clear a support bar defined relative to the full dataset. This is a known issue in ARM applied to imbalanced data \citep{liu1999mining} and is treated as the key finding motivating the next phase of work, rather than a project-ending negative result.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}

Two limitations have been identified and explicitly documented rather than silently worked around:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Median-split discretization} inflates spurious associations and discards magnitude-of-expression information, since every feature is forced to a fixed 50\% support split regardless of its actual distribution.
    \item \textbf{Global support thresholding} under class imbalance systematically suppresses rules for the minority class, as demonstrated directly by the zero \texttt{relapse\_yes} rules obtained in the current experiment.
\end{enumerate}

Both limitations are consistent with, and helpful for demonstrating, the project's first stated objective: characterizing how classical ARM performs on this class of data.

\section{Planned Next Steps}

The following steps are prepared but not yet executed:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Class-relative support thresholding}: Recompute support as a fraction of each class's size independently, rather than as a fraction of the whole dataset, to allow \texttt{relapse\_yes} rules to be discovered on equal footing with \texttt{relapse\_no} rules.
    \item \textbf{Scaling to larger feature subsets}: Repeat the CARM experiments on the top-500 and top-1000 MI-ranked gene subsets to assess how rule quantity, quality, and stability change with dimensionality.
    \item \textbf{ML baseline benchmarking}: Train and evaluate a standard supervised classifier (e.g.\ logistic regression or random forest) on the same feature subsets to provide a predictive-performance baseline against which the ARM-based approach can be compared, directly addressing the project's fourth objective.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

To date, a working two-stage pipeline (cleaning, then feature selection) has been implemented and validated, producing a clean dataset of 286 samples and 22{,}215 expression probes, MI-based gene rankings, and top-$k$ gene subsets. An initial CARM experiment on the top-100 genes surfaced a small number of low-stability rules, all confined to the majority class, and this outcome was traced to a specific, well-understood cause: global support thresholding under class imbalance. This diagnosis directly motivates the next planned step, class-relative support thresholding, and keeps the project on track toward its remaining objectives of scaling and ML benchmarking.
