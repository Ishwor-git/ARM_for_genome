\section{Introduction}
Association Rule Mining (ARM) is a fundamental technique in Knowledge Discovery in Databases (KDD) for identifying relationships and co-occurrence patterns among variables in large datasets. Originally developed for transactional analysis, ARM has become an important pattern-discovery approach because it can identify combinations of features that occur together without requiring a predefined parametric model. Algorithms such as Apriori and FP-Growth made it possible to systematically search large combinatorial spaces for frequent itemsets and derive association rules from them. Although traditionally associated with applications such as market-basket analysis, the ability of ARM to discover multi-feature relationships has motivated its application to more complex domains, including biomedical research and genomics. In these domains, associations among multiple genes, molecular markers, or phenotypic characteristics may provide useful insights that are difficult to capture using analyses focused on individual variables.

Genomic and transcriptomic datasets, however, differ substantially from the transactional datasets for which classical ARM was originally developed. High-throughput technologies such as microarrays can measure the expression levels of thousands of genes across a relatively small number of biological samples, resulting in a high-dimensional setting in which the number of features (p) can greatly exceed the number of observations (n) $((p \gg n))$. Microarray measurements are also continuous and therefore generally require preprocessing or discretization before conventional association-rule algorithms can be applied. Despite these challenges, the structure of such data makes association mining potentially attractive. Biological phenomena may depend on combinations of genes rather than isolated features, and interactions among multiple genes may contain information that is not apparent from univariate analysis. Consequently, ARM provides a natural framework for investigating recurring combinations of gene-expression states and their associations with biological or clinical characteristics.

The same characteristics that make high-dimensional genomic data attractive for pattern mining also create substantial computational and analytical challenges. Classical ARM algorithms operate over a combinatorial search space whose size can increase rapidly with the number of available items. In a microarray dataset containing thousands of genes, even a relatively small fraction of genes can produce an enormous number of possible itemsets. The resulting search can lead to excessive execution time and memory consumption, while stringent minimum-support thresholds may eliminate potentially interesting patterns. Conversely, relaxing the support threshold can cause a rapid increase in the number of frequent itemsets and association rules, producing a large volume of redundant, weak, or difficult-to-interpret patterns. Thus, the central challenge is not simply whether an ARM algorithm can generate rules from genomic data, but whether it can do so within practical computational limits while producing patterns that remain analytically useful.

\section{Dataset and Experimental Setup}

\subsection{Dataset}

The dataset used in this study is a publicly available Affymetrix HG-U133A microarray
gene expression dataset for breast cancer, comprising raw probe-level intensity values
alongside clinical annotations for each patient sample.

\subsubsection{Biological context}
Each sample corresponds to a primary breast tumor profiled at diagnosis, with the
clinical outcome of interest being disease relapse. Gene expression from microarray
probes serves as a proxy for the transcriptional state of the tumor, under the
hypothesis that differential expression of specific genes is associated with relapse
risk. This is a well-studied outcome-prediction problem in the breast cancer genomics
literature, motivating the use of expression-derived rules as an interpretable
complement to black-box classifiers.

\paragraph{AFFX control probes.} The HG-U133A array includes a set of AFFX-prefixed
probes used for platform-level quality control (e.g., spike-in and housekeeping
controls). These do not represent biologically variable gene expression relevant to
relapse and were removed prior to any downstream analysis.

\paragraph{Gene expression (genetic) features.} The bulk of the feature space —
22{,}215 probes after control-probe removal — consists of continuous log-intensity
expression values, each corresponding to a distinct gene or transcript. These are the
primary features of interest and the sole target of the mutual-information ranking
and gene-subset selection described in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\paragraph{Clinical covariates.} Two clinically annotated covariates,
\texttt{lymph\_node\_status} and \texttt{ER\_Status} (estrogen receptor status), were
retained alongside the expression features. Both are established prognostic factors
in breast cancer and were kept to allow the ARM phase to surface rules combining
molecular and clinical evidence, though the current study's ARM results
(Section~\ref{sec:arm-results}) are reported over gene-expression-only itemsets, with
clinical covariate integration noted as future work.

\paragraph{Leakage-risk columns.} Three columns — \texttt{GEO\_accession\_number},
\texttt{time\_to\_relapse}, and \texttt{Brain\_relapses} — were excluded because they
either directly encode outcome-adjacent information (leaking the relapse label) or
carry no generalizable biological signal (a sample identifier). Their removal is
detailed in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}.

\paragraph{Target label.} The \texttt{relapse} column is the binary outcome of
interest. As discussed in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}, this was one-hot encoded
into \texttt{relapse\_yes} / \texttt{relapse\_no} items rather than left as a bare
binary column, a correction that was necessary for the minority class to be mineable
under standard ARM itemization.

\subsection{Data Preprocessing}
\label{sec:preprocessing}
Raw microarray intensity data is continuous, high-dimensional, and heavily
platform-annotated — none of which is directly compatible with classical
transaction-based Association Rule Mining. Converting this data into a valid ARM
input required several deliberate preprocessing decisions, described below.

\subsubsection{Filtering and leakage removal}
AFFX control probes (spike-in controls used for platform quality assessment rather
than biological signal) were removed, as were columns that would leak the outcome
directly: \texttt{GEO\_accession\_number}, \texttt{time\_to\_relapse}, and
\texttt{Brain\_relapses}. Clinical covariates with plausible prognostic value
(\texttt{lymph\_node\_status}, \texttt{ER\_Status}) were retained alongside the
\texttt{relapse} target. This yielded a cleaned matrix of $286 \times 22{,}215$
expression features plus covariates and target.

\subsubsection{Normalization}
No additional normalization was applied beyond the platform-provided
log-intensity values in the source series-matrix file; expression values
were used as-is prior to discretization.

\subsubsection{Missing values}
A completeness check on the merged expression matrix confirmed no missing
measurements across all 286 samples; no imputation was required.

\subsubsection{Gene selection and dimensionality variants}
Given $p = 22{,}215 \gg n = 286$ (a feature-to-sample ratio of roughly
$78{:}1$), exhaustive ARM over the full expression space is computationally
intractable and statistically unreliable. Gene selection was therefore
performed as a supervised filter step prior to mining, using three
class-aware ranking methods computed against the binary \texttt{relapse}
label: mutual information (MI, primary method; scores range $0$--$0.119$,
median $\approx 0.019$), chi-square ($\chi^2$ up to $12.55$, computed on the
same discretized features as MI), and a two-sample $t$-test on the
\emph{continuous} intensities ($|t|$ up to $4.89$), which avoids the
information loss of discretization at the cost of comparing means only.
Following ranking, top-$k$ gene subsets are exported for
$k \in \{100, 150, 200, 250, 300, 350, 400\}$ (the exported sizes are set by
a single constant in the selection script). The ARM experiments reported in
Section~\ref{sec:arm-results} cover all three rankings at $k = 100$ (the
headline subset) and $k = 150$ (the feature-count sensitivity comparison).

\paragraph{Cross-method agreement.} Because the three rankings are computed
on the same discretized matrix, their agreement is directly measurable.
Figure~\ref{fig:feature-overlap} partitions each top-$k$ subset into the
seven disjoint Venn regions for $k = 100$ and $k = 150$. At $k = 100$ only 2
of the 100 genes are selected by all three methods, and the three rankings
together nominate 264 distinct genes. The primary MI ranker shares just 7
genes with the other two methods combined, whereas the chi-square and
$t$-test rankings agree on 29 genes; at $k = 150$ the picture is essentially
unchanged (3 common genes, union 396). The choice of ranker therefore
materially changes the feature set handed to the miner.

\subsubsection{Discretization of continuous expression values}
Each of the 22{,}215 expression probes was converted independently into a
binary item via a per-probe median split, using strict inequality:
\begin{equation}
x_{ij}' = \begin{cases} 1 & x_{ij} > \mathrm{median}(x_{\cdot j}) \\ 0 & \text{otherwise} \end{cases}
\end{equation}
so that values equal to the probe's median fall into the low bin. This
produces an approximately, but not exactly, balanced split per gene (observed
high-bin support in $[0.489, 0.500]$ across selected genes). The split is
unsupervised with respect to \texttt{relapse} and, as noted in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}, discards magnitude information and can
inflate spurious co-occurrences for genes whose values cluster near the
median — a deliberate first-pass baseline rather than a final discretization
method.

\subsubsection{Conversion into transactions and items}
Following discretization, each sample was represented as a transaction —
a set of categorical items — following the template:
\[
\text{Sample}_i = \{\text{Gene}_1\_\text{HIGH},\ \text{Gene}_7\_\text{LOW},\
\text{Gene}_{42}\_\text{HIGH},\ \ldots\}
\]
applied independently to each selected gene subset, producing transaction
databases of corresponding width for the ARM experiments in
Section~\ref{sec:arm-results}.

\subsubsection{Label handling}
The target label required particular care during itemization. An initial
implementation encoded \texttt{relapse} as a single bare binary column,
which under standard itemization structurally suppressed rule discovery on
the minority (relapse) side. This was corrected by one-hot encoding the
target into two explicit items, \texttt{relapse\_yes} and
\texttt{relapse\_no}, each usable as a candidate consequent under the
class-relative support thresholds described in Section~\ref{sec:arm-results}.

\section{Methodology and Pipeline}

This section documents the association-rule mining methodology executed on the
binary matrices produced in Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}. Every stage was
applied independently to the three class-aware feature-selection rankings
(mutual information, chi-square, $t$-test) and to both exported subset sizes,
$k \in \{100, 150\}$, so that the effect of feature-count sensitivity can be
separated from the composition of the selected gene set. All frequent-itemset
mining uses the Apriori implementation in \texttt{mlxtend}
\citep{raschka2018mlxtend}, following the classical Apriori
algorithm \citep{agrawal1994fast}~\citep{agrawal1993mining}; support,
confidence, and lift are computed by the same library.

\subsection{Association rule mining over reduced feature sets}

\paragraph{Input and item encoding.} Each input matrix holds 286 binary
transactions: the $k$ selected genes discretized into high ($1$) / low ($0$)
items plus the target column. The target is re-encoded from the bare binary
\texttt{relapse} column into two mutually exclusive one-hot items,
\texttt{relapse\_yes} and \texttt{relapse\_no}, because a bare binary column
would otherwise only ever produce an item for the positive class and leave the
majority class unrepresentable as a rule consequent (Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}).

\paragraph{Parameters.} Frequent itemsets are mined with minimum support
$0.10$, a maximum itemset length of 3, and association rules are derived from
the frequent itemsets with minimum confidence $0.50$. These thresholds
deliberately favour recall over precision: at $p \gg n$ the primary failure
mode of classical ARM is rule \emph{volume}, and the filtering stages in
Sections~\ref{sec:method-cf} and~\ref{sec:method-classrel} are what separate
signal from noise. Resulting scale and runtimes are reported in
Section~\ref{sec:res-scale}.

\subsection{Class-association rule extraction and quality metrics}
\label{sec:method-cf}

A class-association rule (CAR) is defined as any rule whose consequent is a
single item in $\{\texttt{relapse\_yes},\ \texttt{relapse\_no}\}$; rules with
multi-item or non-class consequents are discarded. Three quality metrics are
used to rank the retained CARs:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Raw statistics}: support, confidence, and lift. A rule's
    antecedent is additionally required to have support at least $0.07$
    ($\approx$20 of 286 samples) so that sample-rare antecedents cannot
    manufacture high-confidence rules.
    \item \textbf{Certainty factor (CF)}: a base-rate-adjusted confidence.
    Letting $s$ denote the consequent's marginal support (the class base
    rate),
    \[
    \mathit{CF} \;=\; \begin{cases}
        (\mathrm{conf} - s)/(1 - s), & \mathrm{conf} \ge s, \\
        (\mathrm{conf} - s)/s,       & \mathrm{conf} < s.
    \end{cases}
    \]
    $\mathit{CF} = 0$ means confidence equals the class base rate (no signal
    beyond chance); $\mathit{CF} = 1$ is perfect confidence on a rare class.
    Because CF is normalized against each consequent's own rate,
    \texttt{relapse\_yes} and \texttt{relapse\_no} rules are directly
    comparable despite the $107{:}179$ class imbalance.
    \item \textbf{Leverage}: $\mathit{support}(A \cup C)
    - \mathit{support}(A)\cdot s$, a co-occurrence (rather than posterior)
    check. Negative leverage flags rules whose joint occurrence is rarer than
    independence would predict and which are therefore not sample-backed.
\end{itemize}

Rules that do not beat the base rate ($\mathit{CF} \le 0$) are dropped in a
post-hoc filter --- without re-mining --- so that only better-than-baseline
rules are reported.

\subsection{Class-relative support thresholding}
\label{sec:method-classrel}

A single global support threshold applied to the full 286-sample dataset
systematically disadvantages the minority class: itemsets confined to the 107
relapse-yes patients must clear a bar defined against the majority base rate.
This is the multiple-minimum-support problem of imbalanced ARM
\citep{liu1999mining}. The methodology therefore mines each class subset
\emph{independently} at a matched \emph{absolute} support: a floor of 20
samples is applied to both classes and converted to a per-subset fraction,
$\mathrm{min\_support} = 20/n_{\mathrm{class}}$, i.e.\ $0.187$ for the 107
relapse-yes patients and $0.112$ for the 179 relapse-no patients. Both classes
thus contribute candidate itemsets on equal footing with respect to their own
size. From the two per-class item-set sets, an itemset is called
\emph{class-exclusive} if it is frequent in one class and occurs in fewer than
20 samples in the other; itemsets frequent in both are \emph{shared}.
Exclusive itemsets are the candidates for class-characteristic signal.

\subsection{Permutation null test for rule significance}
\label{sec:method-perm}

Exclusivity counts grow mechanically with itemset volume, so raw exclusive
counts are not evidence of real class differences. A permutation null test
quantifies how much class-exclusivity would appear \emph{by chance alone}: the
relapse labels are shuffled 20 times, preserving the exact $107{:}179$ class
sizes, and the identical class-relative mining procedure is re-run on each
shuffled matrix. The empirical $p$-value for an observed exclusive count is the
fraction of the 20 null runs whose count reaches or exceeds it. A fixed master
seed (20260806) keeps the shuffle sequence reproducible.

\subsection{Characterization of the validated signal}
\label{sec:method-char}

The class-relative mining output is dominated by three-item itemsets, which
are hard to interpret. To characterize the signal that the permutation test
validates, the relapse-no-exclusive \emph{two-item} itemsets are extracted;
for each pair, the exact support inside each class is recomputed by direct
counting, and the genes participating in many pairs are tallied.

\subsection{Ongoing and incomplete experiments}
\label{sec:method-ongoing}

The following components are part of the designed pipeline but are not yet
executed, and results will be added as they complete:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{FP-Growth evaluation}: re-running the mining stage with
    \texttt{mlxtend}'s FP-Growth implementation to compare runtime and rule
    output against Apriori.
    \item \textbf{Feature counts beyond 150}: only the $k \in \{100, 150\}$
    subsets are exported and mined so far; pushing $k$ toward 200--400 is in
    progress.
    \item \textbf{Supervised baseline}: a standard classifier trained on the
    same reduced feature sets for the benchmark comparison (objective 4).
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Scale and feasibility of classical ARM}
\label{sec:res-scale}

\subsection{Class-association rules and base-rate-adjusted quality}
\label{sec:res-car}

At $k=100$ the class-rule counts differ sharply across selection methods
(Table~\ref{tab:car}): mutual information yields only 107 relapse-yes rules
against 4{,}954 relapse-no rules, chi-square 982/4{,}040, and the $t$-test
1{,}601/3{,}470. In raw counts the majority class dominates
$13$--$46{:}1$ --- the apparent ``zero-minority-rule'' phenomenon. The CF
base-rate correction shows this is largely an artifact: no relapse-yes rule
from any method fails the better-than-baseline test (0 dropped at
$\mathit{CF} \le 0$), whereas 22--39\% of the relapse-no rules are dropped as

genuinely sparse} rather than absent. Every rule that passes the
$\mathit{CF} > 0$ filter has positive leverage --- it reflects a co-occurrence
stronger than chance, not a single-patient artifact.

\subsection{Permutation-based significance}
\label{sec:res-perm}

Table~\ref{tab:perm} reports the empirical permutation $p$-values for the two

\begin{itemize}

    ($p = 0.35$; at $k{=}150$, 6{,}081 in $[2127,\ 11{,}758]$, $p = 0.40$) ---

\subsection{Feature-count sensitivity: $k = 100$ to $k = 150$}
\label{sec:res-sensitivity}

Table~\ref{tab:sens} contrasts the headline metrics at the two subset sizes.
Total rule volume triples for every method, but \emph{quality} --- measured by
permutation significance and validated two-item signal --- behaves
differently:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Chi-square and $t$-test}: the minority-class exclusive signal
    remains permutation-significant ($p = 0.00$) at $k = 150$, and the
    validated relapse-no two-item itemsets grow from 1{,}559 to 3{,}994
    (chi-square) and 992 to 2{,}105 ($t$-test). The extra 50 features buy a
    roughly doubled volume of validated signal without degrading its
    significance.
    \item \textbf{Mutual information}: the additional features increase raw
    volume but \emph{weaken} the validated signal --- the relapse-no-exclusive
    significance drops from $p = 0.00$ to marginal $p = 0.05$ at $k = 150$,
    and the minority class stays non-significant. The two-item itemsets still
    grow 745 $\rightarrow$ 1{,}486, so the loss is in exclusivity significance,
    not in rule volume.
\end{itemize}

In summary, raising the feature count from 100 to 150 multiplies raw output
$3$--$4\times$ but only translates into doubled \emph{validated} signal for
the chi-square and $t$-test rankings; under mutual-information selection the
majority-class exclusivity becomes marginal. This motivates treating
``improvement'' in terms of permutation-validated signal rather than rule
count, and it sets up the planned scaling experiments beyond $k = 150$.

\section{Discussion and Limitations}
\label{sec:limitations}

The executed experiments replace the earlier placeholder narrative (a small
set of low-stability rules confined to the majority class) with a
quantitative picture: classical ARM over the reduced feature sets is fast but
rule-volume-bound, the CF correction shows the minority class is \emph{not}
rule-free but genuinely sparse at high quality, and a permutation test shows
the statistically validated signal is carried by different classes depending
on the feature-selection method. Four limitations of the current experiment
are explicitly recognized:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{Median-split discretization} discards magnitude-of-expression
    information and forces every gene into a near-50/50 split, inflating the
    risk of spurious co-occurrences (Section~\ref{sec:preprocessing}).
    \item \textbf{Feature selection and evaluation share the same cohort}: the
    three rankings, the class-relative mining, and the permutation test all
    operate on the same 286 samples. The permutation null is internal and does
    not test generalization; an external cohort would be required to confirm
    the class-specific signals.
    \item \textbf{Permutation resolution}: 20 shuffles give a $p$-value
    granularity of 0.05. ``$p = 0.00$'' means that no null run reached the
    observed count, and conclusions at the 0.05 level should be read as
    indicative rather than exact.
    \item \textbf{Class-imbalance effects}: the majority-class advantage seen
    in raw rule counts is a base-rate artifact; after the CF correction,
    minority-class rules exist but their quality degrades sharply at high CF,
    so they are sparse rather than absent.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Planned Next Steps}

The planned next steps follow directly from the results and the documented
shortfalls:

\begin{enumerate}
    \item \textbf{FP-Growth evaluation}: re-run the mining stage with
    \texttt{mlxtend}'s FP-Growth to compare itemset output and runtime against
    the Apriori numbers in Table~\ref{tab:apriori-scale}.
    \item \textbf{Scaling beyond 150 features}: export and mine the
    $k \in \{200,\ldots,400\}$ subsets (subsets already produced by the
    feature-selection script) to find where validated-signal quality and
    runtime break down.
    \item \textbf{ML baseline benchmarking}: train a standard supervised
    classifier (e.g.\ logistic regression or a decision tree) on the same
    reduced feature sets to provide a predictive baseline against which the
    rule-based approach can be compared (objective 4).
    \item \textbf{Rule stability}: bootstrap resampling of the retained CARs to
    quantify how the top rules (e.g., the relapse-no-exclusive pairs in
    Table~\ref{tab:twoitem}) survive resampling.
\end{enumerate}

\section{Conclusion}

A working end-to-end pipeline --- cleaning, discretization, class-aware feature
selection, classical Apriori-based class-association mining, base-rate-adjusted
rule filtering, class-relative thresholding, and a permutation null test ---
has been executed on the 286-sample breast-cancer microarray dataset, for all
three feature-selection rankings and both top-100 and top-150 subsets. Apriori
is computationally tractable but produces hundreds of thousands of rules that
must be filtered by class-relative quality metrics. The CF correction resolves
the apparent absence of minority-class rules into genuine sparseness, and the
permutation test shows that the statistically validated signal is
method-dependent: mutual information supports a majority-class
(relapse-no) signal, while chi-square and the $t$-test support a
minority-class (relapse-yes) signal. Moving from 100 to 150 features roughly
doubles the validated two-item signal for the chi-square and $t$-test rankings
while degrading mutual information's majority-class significance, demonstrating
that the value of additional features must be assessed by validated signal
count, not raw rule volume. FP-Growth comparison, higher feature counts, and an
ML baseline remain as advertised follow-on work.
